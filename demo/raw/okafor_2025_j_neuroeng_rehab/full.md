## Abstract

Caffeine is widely used to counteract the cognitive cost of short sleep, but the optimal dose for prefrontal engagement is disputed. We compared 100, 200 and 400 mg caffeine in 32+16 adults restricted to 5 h time in bed for two nights, measuring DLPFC hbO with fNIRS during a 30-min sustained-attention (psychomotor vigilance) task. Dose affected the DLPFC hbO response ({S['F48']}): 200 mg produced the largest increase (+0.24 ± 0.08 µM·cm), exceeding 100 mg (+0.11 ± 0.07 µM·cm), whereas 400 mg did not improve on 200 mg (+0.19 ± 0.09 µM·cm) and was accompanied by self-reported jitter. We conclude that the prefrontal hemodynamic response to caffeine after partial sleep restriction follows an inverted-U dose-response with a maximum near 200 mg.

## Methods

Forty-eight healthy adults (24 female; 18-30 years; habitual caffeine 100-300 mg/day) were randomized in equal groups to 100 mg (n = 16), 200 mg (n = 16) or 400 mg (n = 16) caffeine after two nights of 5 h time in bed. A 30-min psychomotor vigilance task began 45 min post-dose. fNIRS acquisition followed the same 22-channel prefrontal montage and preprocessing pipeline as Chen et al. (2024); the primary outcome was mean DLPFC hbO change from pre-dose baseline across the task. Secondary outcomes were mean 1/RT and self-reported side effects on a 12-item checklist. The dose effect was tested with one-way repeated-measures-corrected ANOVA on dose group with participant as the unit (dosage as between-subject factor); eta-squared partial is reported. The study was preregistered; analysts were blinded to dose labels until after the primary test.

## Results

Primary hemodynamic outcome. Dose affected the DLPFC hbO response ({S['F48']}): 200 mg produced the largest increase (+0.24 ± 0.08 µM·cm), exceeding 100 mg (+0.11 ± 0.07 µM·cm), whereas 400 mg did not improve on 200 mg (+0.19 ± 0.09 µM·cm) and was accompanied by self-reported jitter. Pairwise contrasts confirmed 200 mg > 100 mg (p = .004) and 200 mg > 400 mg (p = .031); 400 mg vs. 100 mg did not differ (p = .21).

Behavior. Mean 1/RT followed the same ranking (100 mg 2.71 s⁻¹, 200 mg 3.02 s⁻¹, 400 mg 2.88 s⁻¹) but the omnibus test was p = .07.

Side effects. Self-reported jitter was more frequent in the 400 mg group (9/16) than in the 200 mg (2/16) and 100 mg (0/16) groups.

## Discussion

The prefrontal hemodynamic benefit of caffeine after partial sleep restriction peaks at an intermediate dose: 200 mg maximized DLPFC hbO, whereas 400 mg added nothing hemodynamically and doubled the rate of subjective jitter. Together with behavioral trends in the same direction, the data support an inverted-U dose-response for prefrontal engagement. Because the design is between-subjects, individual differences in caffeine metabolism contribute unmodelled variance; and the 5 h restriction protocol limits generalization to total sleep deprivation.

## Conclusion

After partial sleep restriction, the caffeine dose-response of prefrontal hemodynamic engagement is non-monotonic, peaking near 200 mg and declining at 400 mg.
