## Abstract

Most demonstrations of caffeine's cognitive benefits come from sleep-restricted or fatigued samples, leaving the rested-state effect uncertain. In a double-blind crossover study, 32 fully rested adults performed 2-back and 3-back working-memory tasks after 200 mg caffeine and after placebo with concurrent prefrontal fNIRS. Caffeine produced no reliable change in accuracy or DLPFC hbO in either task. In fully rested participants, 200 mg caffeine produced no change in 3-back accuracy relative to placebo (t(30) = 0.81, p = .424, d = 0.09). These null results bound the scope of caffeine's working-memory effect: benefits reported after sleep loss do not extend to rested participants under matched task conditions.

## Methods

Participants were 32 healthy adults (16 female; 19-33 years), all verified as sleeping 7-9 h per night by actigraphy during the 7 days before each session (mean 7.6 h). Each completed two sessions (200 mg caffeine, placebo), counterbalanced, ≥7 days apart, starting 08:30 after a normal night's sleep. The 2-back and 3-back tasks (10 min each) began 60 min post-dose, with the same 22-channel prefrontal fNIRS montage as Chen et al. (2024). Primary outcomes were 3-back accuracy and left-DLPFC hbO change from pre-dose baseline, analyzed with paired t-tests.

## Results

Behavior. In fully rested participants, 200 mg caffeine produced no change in 3-back accuracy relative to placebo (t(30) = 0.81, p = .424, d = 0.09). The 2-back showed the same null pattern (t(30) = 0.35, p = .730).

Hemodynamics. Left-DLPFC hbO change did not differ between caffeine and placebo (+0.03 ± 0.06 vs. +0.02 ± 0.07 µM·cm; t(30) = 0.57, p = .573). No prefrontal region showed a session difference (all p > .4).

Sensitivity. A Bayes factor analysis of the primary accuracy contrast gave BF01 = 4.6, moderate evidence for the null.

## Discussion

In participants sleeping 7-9 h nightly, verified by actigraphy, a standard 200 mg dose left neither working-memory performance nor prefrontal hemodynamics changed. Combined with reports that caffeine rescues performance after sleep restriction or deprivation, the present null pattern suggests the benefit is a restoration of degraded function rather than an enhancement above the rested baseline — and that studies sampling only rested participants should not expect replicable working-memory gains.

## Conclusion

Under fully rested conditions, 200 mg caffeine does not change working-memory performance or prefrontal hemodynamic activation.
