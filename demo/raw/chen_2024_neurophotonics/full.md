## Abstract

Sleep deprivation degrades working memory and reduces prefrontal cortical oxygenation, yet the degree to which a moderate dose of caffeine repairs both the behavioral and the hemodynamic deficit remains unclear. In a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled crossover design, 24 healthy adults (18-34 years) underwent 24 h of total sleep deprivation and performed a 3-back working-memory task 60 min after ingesting 200 mg caffeine or placebo, while hemodynamic changes in the prefrontal cortex were recorded with a 22-channel continuous-wave fNIRS system. Caffeine improved 3-back accuracy relative to placebo and increased oxyhemoglobin (hbO) concentration in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. The behavioral benefit tracked the hemodynamic change across participants. These results indicate that, after total sleep deprivation, caffeine partially rescues both the performance and the prefrontal hemodynamic correlates of working memory.

## Methods

Participants were 24 healthy, non-smoking adults (12 female; mean age 24.6 years) recruited from a university community, with habitual caffeine intake below 200 mg/day verified by a 7-day diary. Each participant completed two 48-h laboratory sessions (caffeine, placebo) separated by at least 6 days, in counterbalanced order. Total sleep deprivation (24 h of enforced wakefulness under constant routine supervision) began at 07:00. At 06:00 the following morning, participants received an opaque capsule containing either 200 mg caffeine or microcrystalline cellulose. The 3-back task (10 min, three blocks of 60 letters) started 60 min post-dose. Task order and stimulus lists were identical across sessions.

Hemodynamic data were acquired with a 22-channel continuous-wave fNIRS system (source-detector separation 3 cm; sampling rate 10 Hz) covering bilateral prefrontal cortex. Concentration changes in oxyhemoglobin (hbO) and deoxyhemoglobin (HbR) were computed with the modified Beer-Lambert law and averaged over the 10-min task block relative to a 5-min pre-dose baseline. Channels were assigned to left/right DLPFC and medial PFC regions of interest following a standard 10-5 layout. Motion artifacts were corrected with temporal correlation filtering, and trials with residual correlation below 0.8 were discarded.

Statistical analysis used paired t-tests comparing sessions (caffeine vs. placebo) for accuracy and for mean hbO change in each region of interest, with Cohen's d for repeated measures reported as effect size; the behavior-hemodynamics association was tested with a Pearson correlation between per-participant accuracy gain and per-participant left-DLPFC hbO gain. The alpha level was set at .05, two-tailed.

## Results

Behavior. Across participants, 3-back accuracy was higher after caffeine than after placebo (85.7 ± 5.9% vs. 78.2 ± 6.4%; t(23) = 4.21, p < .001, d = 0.86). Response times showed the same pattern (mean RT 689 ± 91 ms after caffeine vs. 741 ± 88 ms after placebo), but this analysis focuses on accuracy.

Hemodynamics. Caffeine reversed the sleep-deprivation-related decrease in left DLPFC hbO (change from pre-dose baseline +0.28 ± 0.09 µM·cm under caffeine vs. -0.05 ± 0.11 µM·cm under placebo), and the behavioral improvement correlated with the hemodynamic change across participants (r = 0.61, p = .002). Right DLPFC showed the same direction of effect at smaller magnitude (+0.14 ± 0.10 µM·cm); medial PFC and HbR changes did not differ between sessions (all p > .15).

Behavior-hemodynamics coupling. Participants with larger left-DLPFC hbO gains showed larger accuracy gains (r = 0.61, p = .002), consistent with a coupled rescue of performance and prefrontal oxygenation.

## Discussion

The main finding is that 200 mg caffeine, administered after 24 h of total sleep deprivation, partially restored 3-back accuracy and left-DLPFC hbO in the same participants, and the two recoveries covaried. The effect size for the behavioral benefit was large (t(23) = 4.21, p < .001, d = 0.86), and the cross-participant coupling suggests the hemodynamic measure is a plausible surrogate marker of the functional rescue. Limitations include the modest sample, a single time point 60 min post-dose, and coverage restricted to prefrontal cortex; occipital and parietal contributions could not be assessed.

## Conclusion

Under total sleep deprivation, a moderate caffeine dose partially restores both working-memory performance and prefrontal hemodynamic activation, and the two effects are coupled across individuals.
